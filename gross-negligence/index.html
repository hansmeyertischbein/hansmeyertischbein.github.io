Careless or grossly careless? When your insurer can cut the payout

Most claims are paid in full even when the damage was your own fault. The exception is gross negligence. Here is how Norwegian insurance law draws the line, explained with two real cases from the complaints board.

  • Simulating a year of claims

The rule in short

Insurance exists to cover accidents, and most accidents involve someone being careless. Ordinary carelessness therefore does not reduce what you get paid. The Insurance Contracts Act (forsikringsavtaleloven) sets out three levels in § 4-9, which applies to property and other non-life insurance:

  • Ordinary negligence: no effect on the payout, beyond your normal excess.
  • Gross negligence (grov uaktsomhet): the insurer may reduce the payout, or in serious cases refuse it.
  • Intent (forsett): the insurer is not liable.

Similar rules for personal insurance, such as accident insurance, are found in §§ 13-8 and 13-9.

Where the line goes

The Supreme Court has described gross negligence as a marked deviation from ordinary prudent conduct: behaviour that is strongly blameworthy, considerably more so than ordinary negligence. The test has been repeated since the late 1980s and confirmed in HR-2017-1977-A.

The same factors are used as when judging ordinary carelessness, but more is required. The complaints board typically asks three questions:

  • How large was the risk of damage?
  • Was the risk visible to you at the time?
  • Did you have a simple, safer alternative?

If the answer is gross negligence, the size of the cut depends on the degree of fault, how the damage happened and the circumstances in general. It is a judgement, not a table. In cases brought before the complaints board, insurers have cut payouts by anything from 15% to 100%.

Two cases, two outcomes

The child in the back seat (FinKN-2020-848). A driver at 40 km/h in a 50 zone turned briefly when his two-year-old shouted from the back seat and hit a concrete barrier. The insurer cut the payout by 15% for gross negligence. The board disagreed: a reflex reaction to a child calling out, lasting a few seconds at low speed, is not a marked deviation from prudent conduct. No reduction.

The dropped glove (FinKN-2026-607). A driver at 60 km/h leaned down to pick up a glove from the floor on the passenger side, ran off the road and hit a tree. The insurer cut the payout by 20%. The board agreed. Leaning down was an active choice, not a moment’s inattention, it was completely unnecessary, and there were houses and a footpath nearby. The 20% reduction stood.

The difference is not how long the driver looked away. It is whether the lapse was an understandable reflex or a choice that could easily have waited. That is the practical meaning of a marked deviation.

Who has to prove what

It is the insurer that must show gross negligence, not you who must show you were careful. The standard is the ordinary civil one: it must be more likely than not, as the Supreme Court confirmed in HR-2016-2579-A. What you say when you first report the claim is often central evidence, so describe what happened accurately and calmly.

A related rule that works differently

Many policies contain safety requirements, such as locking doors and windows, keeping certain items out of sight in a car, or turning off water when a holiday home is empty. Breaking such a requirement is handled under a separate provision on safety regulations (sikkerhetsforskrifter, § 4-8), not as gross negligence. The outcome can be similar, a reduced payout, but the questions asked are different, so it matters which rule the insurer relies on.

If your payout is cut

  • Ask for the decision in writing, with the rule it is based on and why the size of the reduction was chosen.
  • Check whether the insurer relies on gross negligence (§ 4-9) or a safety requirement in the terms (§ 4-8).
  • Complain to the insurer first, with any facts you think were missed.
  • If you still disagree, take the case to Finansklagenemnda, the independent complaints board for financial services, as both drivers above did.

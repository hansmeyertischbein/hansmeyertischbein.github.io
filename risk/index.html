Forecasting risk on Oslo Børs with machine learning

Can machine learning predict how much Norwegian stocks will move next month, and which ones are about to fall sharply, better than the simple models the industry uses? A test on almost twelve years of data, with every model trained only on what was known at the time, and new forecasts every month.

Test period Jan 2015 – Aug 2026Updated on the 1st of each monthNot investment advice

Risk measures

Volatility
How much a share price moves up and down. Measured as the standard deviation of daily returns. A stock with 30% volatility typically moves about 30% up or down over a year, or about 2% on an ordinary day.
Realised volatility
The volatility that actually happened over a period, calculated from the daily returns in that period. Here it is the target: the volatility over the next 21 trading days, which the models try to forecast.
Annualised
Scaled to a one-year figure so that numbers over different periods can be compared. Daily volatility is multiplied by the square root of 252, the number of trading days in a year.
Log volatility
The natural logarithm of volatility. The models forecast it in logs because volatility is always positive and skewed; in logs, going from 20% to 40% counts the same as going from 10% to 20%.
Horizon, 21 trading days
The forecast period, about one calendar month of trading.
Large fall
A share closing at least 15% below its month-end price at some point in the next 21 trading days. The test also uses 10% and 20% to check that the results do not depend on the threshold.
Intraday range
The gap between a day’s highest and lowest price. Averaged over a month, it measures volatility using more information than closing prices alone (the Parkinson estimator).
Downside volatility
Volatility calculated only from days when the price fell.
Skewness
Whether large moves tend to be up or down. Negative skewness means large falls are more common than large rises.
Fat tails, kurtosis
How often extreme days happen compared with a normal distribution. Fat tails mean more very large moves than a bell curve would suggest.
Beta
How much a stock tends to move when the whole market moves. A beta of 1.5 means it typically moves 1.5% when the market moves 1%.
Stock-specific volatility
The part of a stock’s volatility that the market does not explain, for example company news.
Fall from 52-week high
How far the price is below its highest close over the last year.
Value at Risk, VaR 95%
A loss that should be exceeded only one month in twenty. A one-month VaR of −8% means that in 95% of months the share should not fall more than 8%.
VaR breach, exceedance
A month in which the loss was larger than the VaR. If the VaR is right, this happens in about 5% of cases.

Data

OSEBX
Oslo Børs Benchmark Index, the main index of the Norwegian stock market. It includes dividends.
Universe
The stocks included in a given month. Here: stocks with at least a year of history, a median daily turnover of at least NOK 5 million and a share price of at least NOK 1, decided at each month end.
Turnover (trading)
The value of shares traded in a day: price times number of shares.
Liquidity, Amihud illiquidity
How easily a share can be traded without moving the price. Amihud’s measure is the average price move per krone traded; a high value means an illiquid stock.
Adjusted close
A closing price corrected for dividends and splits, so that returns are not distorted when a company pays out cash or changes its number of shares.
Split
When a company divides each share into several, or merges several into one. The price changes, but the value of the holding does not.
Survivorship bias
The error that comes from only looking at companies that still exist. Companies that went bankrupt or were taken over are missing, so returns look better and risk looks lower than they really were.
Look-ahead bias
Using information that was not available at the time of the forecast. Even a few hours can matter: VIX and oil close after Oslo Børs, so they are lagged by one day here.
Cross-check, second source
Comparing the data with an independent source to catch errors. Here Yahoo Finance prices are compared with Euronext, the company that runs Oslo Børs.
ISIN
A twelve-character international code that identifies a security, for example NO0010096985 for Equinor.
Sector dummies
Yes/no inputs that tell the model which industry a company belongs to, such as energy, shipping or seafood.
GitHub Action
A program that runs automatically on GitHub’s servers on a schedule. Here it fetches the data and makes new forecasts on the first day of each month.

Market data used as inputs

VIX
An index of the volatility that US option prices imply for the S&P 500 over the next month. Often called the fear index.
Brent
The benchmark price for North Sea crude oil, important for many Norwegian companies.
Policy rate
Norges Bank’s key interest rate, the rate banks get on deposits in the central bank.
USD/NOK
The price of one US dollar in Norwegian kroner. A rise means a weaker krone.

Models

EWMA
Exponentially weighted moving average. Today’s volatility estimate, where recent days count more than older days. Used in the RiskMetrics system since 1996. Needs no fitting.
HAR
Heterogeneous autoregressive model (Corsi, 2009). A linear regression that forecasts volatility from volatility over the last day, week, month and quarter. Simple, and known to be hard to beat. The main reference here.
Reference model, benchmark
A simple model that a new model must beat to be worth using.
Machine learning
Models that learn patterns from data without the relationships being written down in advance, and that can capture non-linear effects and interactions.
LightGBM, gradient boosting
Builds many small decision trees one after another, each one correcting the errors of the trees before it.
Random forest
Builds hundreds of decision trees on random subsets of the data and inputs, and averages their answers.
Decision tree
A model that splits the data step by step with yes/no questions, for example “is last month’s volatility above 30%?”.
Neural network, MLP
A model of layers of simple units that transform the inputs step by step. Here: two hidden layers, averaged over five versions with different random starting points.
ML average, ensemble
The average forecast of LightGBM, random forest and the neural network. Averaging different models usually reduces errors.
Logistic regression
A regression for yes/no outcomes that gives a probability between 0 and 1. Here the reference model for large falls, using two volatility measures.
Two-step model
First the machine learning model forecasts volatility; then a logistic regression turns that forecast into a probability of a large fall.
Input, feature
A piece of information the model uses, such as last month’s volatility or the oil price change.
Settings, hyperparameters
Choices about a model’s structure that are not learned from the data directly, such as the number of leaves in each tree. Chosen on held-out data.
Early stopping
Stopping training when the model stops improving on held-out data, to avoid learning noise.
Overfitting
When a model learns random noise in the training data and therefore does worse on new data.
Calibration
Whether the probabilities mean what they say. If the model says 20% for a hundred stocks, about twenty of them should fall.
Platt scaling, recalibration
Adjusting a model’s probabilities with a small logistic regression fitted on held-out data, to make them better calibrated.
SHAP values
A method that splits each forecast into contributions from each input. The average size of the contributions shows which inputs the model relies on most.

Testing and evaluation

Out of sample
Tested on data the model has not seen during training. The only kind of test that says how a model would have done in real life.
Walk-forward test
For each year, the models are trained only on data from before that year and then tested on the year. Then the window moves forward a year and it is repeated.
Held-out block, validation
The last two years of each training window, kept aside to choose settings and calibrate the models, so that these choices are not made on the data they are later tested on.
Test period
January 2015 to August 2026, the months used to measure how good the forecasts are.
Stock-month
One stock in one month: one forecast and one outcome.
MSE, mean squared error
The average of the squared forecast errors. Lower is better. Here measured on log volatility.
QLIKE
A loss function for variance forecasts that punishes forecasting too little risk more than forecasting too much. Standard in volatility research (Patton, 2011).
Variance
Volatility squared.
Diebold–Mariano test
A test of whether two forecasting models are equally accurate. A small p-value means the difference is unlikely to be chance.
Newey–West standard errors
A way of measuring uncertainty that allows errors in neighbouring months to be correlated, which would otherwise make results look more certain than they are.
p-value
The probability of seeing a difference at least this large if there were really no difference. Below 0.05 is usually called statistically significant.
Statistically significant
Unlikely to be due to chance, given the test and the data. It says nothing about whether the difference is large or useful.
Rank correlation
How well the model puts stocks in the right order from calm to volatile, from −1 to 1. Spearman’s correlation, averaged over months.
AUC
Area under the ROC curve. The probability that the model gives a higher risk to a stock that fell than to one that did not. 0.5 is guessing, 1 is perfect.
Brier score
The average squared difference between the forecast probability and what happened (1 for a fall, 0 otherwise). Lower is better.
Brier skill
How much better the Brier score is than always forecasting the historical fall rate. 0% means no better, negative means worse.
Log loss
A score for probabilities that punishes confident wrong forecasts heavily. Lower is better.
Base rate
How often the event happens on average. About 9% of stock-months had a 15% fall.
Top-10% hit rate
Of the tenth of stocks the model rates riskiest each month, the share that actually fell.
Decile
One of ten equal groups after sorting, for example by forecast probability. D1 is the safest tenth, D10 the riskiest.
Bootstrap, month-block
Measuring uncertainty by redrawing the data at random thousands of times and recalculating the result. Whole months are drawn together, because stocks in the same month move together.
95% interval
The range that contains the true difference with 95% confidence. If it does not include zero, the difference is unlikely to be chance.
Kupiec test
A test of whether the VaR is breached as often as it should be, for example 5% of the time for a 95% VaR.
Stress periods
The Covid crash (February–May 2020) and the energy and rates shock of 2022, shown separately because models often fail when conditions change suddenly.

Portfolio

Low-risk portfolio
The ten stocks with the lowest forecast volatility, in equal amounts, bought at each month end and held for a month.
Equal weight
The same amount invested in each stock.
Rebalancing
Updating the portfolio to the new selection, here at each month end.
Turnover (portfolio)
The share of the portfolio replaced each month. High turnover means higher trading costs.
Trading costs
The cost of buying and selling, here assumed to be 0.2% of each trade.
Annual return
The average yearly growth of an investment, compounded.
Sharpe ratio
Return above the risk-free rate divided by volatility. Return per unit of risk; higher is better.
Risk-free rate
The return on a safe investment, here approximated by Norges Bank’s policy rate.
Worst fall, maximum drawdown
The largest fall from a peak to a later low in the value of the portfolio.
Growth of NOK 1
What one krone invested at the start would have grown to.

The live forecasts

Official forecast
Made from the close on the last trading day of a month, saved before the outcome is known, and never changed. Only these count in the track record.
Preview
A forecast made in the middle of a month. It already knows part of the month, so it is shown but never scored.
Track record
The score of every official forecast once the outcome is known, compared with HAR.
Not investment advice
The forecasts are statistical estimates for research and learning, and can be badly wrong.

      Volatility: does machine learning beat the simple models?

      What worked

      • The two tree models, random forest and LightGBM, both beat HAR clearly, and the average of the three models beat HAR in eleven of twelve years.
      • The intraday range (high against low) turned out to be the single most useful input, more than any of the close-to-close measures HAR uses.

      What didn’t, and what we did

      • The neural network was no better than HAR. It is kept in the average because removing it after seeing the results would be choosing with hindsight; the average still wins.
      • In 2022, when energy prices and interest rates jumped, machine learning was slightly worse than HAR, and in the Covid crash months it was no better. The live version retrains every month instead of once a year, to adapt faster.

      Large falls: can they be seen coming?

      The second question is whether a stock will fall at least 15% below its month-end price at some point in the next month. That happened in 9% of stock-months in the test period, so a model is useful only if it can pick out the riskiest stocks better than chance.

      AUC: the probability that the model ranks a stock that fell above one that did not (0.5 is guessing). Brier skill: how much the probabilities improve on always guessing the historical rate. Top-10% hit rate: share of the riskiest tenth each month that actually fell.

      Stock-months sorted into ten equal groups by forecast probability, from the two-step model. If the probabilities were perfect, the two lines would lie on top of each other.

      What worked

      • A two-step model: feed the machine learning volatility forecast into a one-variable logistic regression. It ranks falls better than the reference at all three thresholds, and the gain at 15% is unlikely to be luck.

      What didn’t, and what we did

      • Machine learning trained directly on falls did not beat a plain logistic regression on two volatility measures. Recalibrating its probabilities (Platt scaling) and adding sectors did not change that. The solution was the two-step model above: the gain from machine learning comes through the better volatility forecast.
      • The probabilities are too high for the riskiest tenth of stocks. This is not solved; the page shows it instead of hiding it.
      • No model could tell which stocks would fall most in the Covid crash of 2020, when almost everything fell. That is a limit of any model built on the recent past.

              Data and method

              • Data. Daily prices and turnover from Yahoo Finance, checked against Euronext. OSEBX, VIX, Brent, USD/NOK and EUR/NOK from Yahoo, and the policy rate from Norges Bank.
              • Universe. . About 41 stocks a month in the test period, 61 in total.
              • Targets. Realised volatility over the next 21 trading days, annualised; and whether the close falls at least 15% below the month-end close within those 21 days.
              • Inputs. 32 inputs known at the month end: volatility over several horizons, the intraday range, downside volatility, skewness, past returns, the fall from the 52-week high, beta, liquidity, market volatility, VIX, oil, the krone and the policy rate, plus sector.
              • Models. LightGBM (gradient boosting), random forest and a neural network (two hidden layers, five seeds averaged), and their average. References: HAR and EWMA for volatility; a logistic regression on two volatility measures for falls.
              • Training. Walk-forward: for each test year, the models are trained on all data whose outcome was known before the year started. The last two years of each training window are held out to choose LightGBM settings and to fit scaling and calibration. The live model does the same every month.
              • Evaluation. Diebold–Mariano tests with Newey–West standard errors, AUC with a month-block bootstrap, Brier skill, calibration by decile, and Kupiec’s test for the VaR.

              This is a research project, not investment advice. The forecasts are statistical estimates that can be badly wrong, especially in crises. Past results do not guarantee future results.

Forecasting risk on Oslo Børs with machine learning

Can machine learning predict how much Norwegian stocks will move next month, and which ones are about to fall sharply, better than the simple models the industry uses? A test on almost twelve years of data, with every model trained only on what was known at the time, and new forecasts every month.

      Volatility: does machine learning beat the simple models?

      Each model forecasts the volatility of every stock for the next month. The reference is HAR, a regression on volatility over the last day, week, month and quarter, which is hard to beat in the research literature. EWMA, the RiskMetrics rule, is included as an even simpler reference.

      What worked

      • The two tree models, random forest and LightGBM, both beat HAR clearly, and the average of the three models beat HAR in eleven of twelve years.
      • The intraday range (high against low) turned out to be the single most useful input, more than any of the close-to-close measures HAR uses.

      What didn’t, and what we did

      • The neural network was no better than HAR. It is kept in the average because removing it after seeing the results would be choosing with hindsight; the average still wins.
      • In 2022, when energy prices and interest rates jumped, machine learning was slightly worse than HAR, and in the Covid crash months it was no better. The live version retrains every month instead of once a year, to adapt faster.

      Large falls: can they be seen coming?

      The second question is whether a stock will fall at least 15% below its month-end price at some point in the next month. That happened in 9% of stock-months in the test period, so a model is useful only if it can pick out the riskiest stocks better than chance.

      AUC: the probability that the model ranks a stock that fell above one that did not (0.5 is guessing). Brier skill: how much the probabilities improve on always guessing the historical rate. Top-10% hit rate: share of the riskiest tenth each month that actually fell.

      What worked

      • A two-step model: feed the machine learning volatility forecast into a one-variable logistic regression. It ranks falls better than the reference at all three thresholds, and the gain at 15% is unlikely to be luck.

      What didn’t, and what we did

      • Machine learning trained directly on falls did not beat a plain logistic regression on two volatility measures. Recalibrating its probabilities (Platt scaling) and adding sectors did not change that. The solution was the two-step model above: the gain from machine learning comes through the better volatility forecast.
      • The probabilities are too high for the riskiest tenth of stocks. This is not solved; the page shows it instead of hiding it.
      • No model could tell which stocks would fall most in the Covid crash of 2020, when almost everything fell. That is a limit of any model built on the recent past.

              Data and method

              • Data. Daily prices and turnover from Yahoo Finance, checked against Euronext. OSEBX, VIX, Brent, USD/NOK and EUR/NOK from Yahoo, and the policy rate from Norges Bank.
              • Universe. . About 41 stocks a month in the test period, 61 in total.
              • Targets. Realised volatility over the next 21 trading days, annualised; and whether the close falls at least 15% below the month-end close within those 21 days.
              • Inputs. 32 inputs known at the month end: volatility over several horizons, the intraday range, downside volatility, skewness, past returns, the fall from the 52-week high, beta, liquidity, market volatility, VIX, oil, the krone and the policy rate, plus sector.
              • Models. LightGBM (gradient boosting), random forest and a neural network (two hidden layers, five seeds averaged), and their average. References: HAR and EWMA for volatility; a logistic regression on two volatility measures for falls.
              • Training. Walk-forward: for each test year, the models are trained on all data whose outcome was known before the year started. The last two years of each training window are held out to choose LightGBM settings and to fit scaling and calibration. The live model does the same every month.
              • Evaluation. Diebold–Mariano tests with Newey–West standard errors, AUC with a month-block bootstrap, Brier skill, calibration by decile, and Kupiec’s test for the VaR.

              This is a research project, not investment advice. The forecasts are statistical estimates that can be badly wrong, especially in crises. Past results do not guarantee future results.

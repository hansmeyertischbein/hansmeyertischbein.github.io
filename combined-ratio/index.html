Who makes money on insurance itself?

The combined ratio shows whether an insurer earns money on underwriting before any investment income. Here is how the three listed Nordic groups have done since the new IFRS 17 accounting standard came into force in 2023.

The measure

The combined ratio is claims plus operating costs, divided by insurance revenue. Below 100% the insurer makes a profit on the insurance itself; above 100% it pays out more than it takes in and relies on investment income to break even. The ratio splits into a loss ratio (claims) and a cost ratio (running the business), so it shows whether a change comes from pricing and risk or from efficiency.

For a Nordic insurer the combined ratio matters more than for many others, because the investment return on premiums held before claims are paid is modest compared with the margin on underwriting. A single point of combined ratio is worth a lot: in 2025 Gjensidige’s insurance revenue was about 1.6 times its equity, so one point of combined ratio moved pre-tax return on equity by roughly 1.6 points.

What stands out

Gjensidige has improved the most. Its combined ratio fell from 87.1% in 2023 to 83.4% in 2025 and 79.1% in the first half of 2026. The company points to price increases across its portfolios, a lower underlying claims frequency and strict cost control. Its cost ratio of about 12% is low for the industry, which is a structural advantage rather than a one-off.

Tryg has been the steadiest, until a court ruling. Tryg improved year by year to 80.3% in 2025. In the first half of 2026 it reported 86.4%, but 80.7% when adjusted for a one-off provision after the Danish Supreme Court ruling on workers’ compensation in April 2026. The same ruling cost Gjensidige NOK 419 million in the second quarter, which shows how a single legal decision can move results across a market.

Sampo is the most stable. The group behind If has stayed between 83.6% and 84.6% in every period, inside its own target of below 85%. Its figure includes If, Topdanmark and the UK insurer Hastings, and the UK business runs at a higher combined ratio than the Nordic one, which lifts the group average.

Read with care

  • The companies define and split their ratios differently. Sampo reports a cost ratio around 25% while Gjensidige reports around 12%, which says as much about definitions and business mix as about efficiency. Compare trends within each company more than levels across them.
  • Under IFRS 17, claims reserves are discounted, so higher interest rates flatter the combined ratio compared with the old standard. Figures before 2023 are not comparable and are left out.
  • Half-year figures are seasonal. Nordic winters, storms and large single claims can move a quarter by several points.
  • A low combined ratio is not automatically good for customers or the company. It can mean strong pricing that later costs volume, or reserve releases that will not repeat.
  • Gjensidige: interim reports Q4 2024, Q4 2025 and Q2 2026.
  • Tryg: annual reports 2024 and 2025, interim report Q2 and H1 2026.
  • Sampo: results for 2024 and 2025, half-year financial report January–June 2026.

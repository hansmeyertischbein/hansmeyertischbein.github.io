Do not short yesterday’s winners

A simple daily strategy on Oslo Børs: when an OSEBX stock rises at least 5% in a day, short it at the next morning’s open and buy it back at that day’s close. It looks brilliant over five years, but almost all of it came from one year, 2021, and trading costs eat the rest.

The rules

  1. Day 0, after the close. Find every OSEBX stock that rose at least 5% that day.
  2. Day 1, at the open. Short all of them, splitting the money equally.
  3. Day 1, at the close. Buy them back. The position is never held overnight.
  4. No signal, no trade. On days when no stock rose that much, the money stays in cash.

The return on one day

Rt = 1Nt ∑i ∈ St Oi,t − Ci,tOi,t − 2c
Rt = 0 on days with no signal, and the money grows as Wt = Wt−1 × (1 + Rt).
St
the stocks that rose at least the threshold the day before: Ci,t−1 / Ci,t−2 − 1 ≥ 5%, using closes adjusted for dividends and splits
Nt
how many stocks are in St; the money is split equally between them
Oi,t, Ci,t
the stock’s opening and closing price on the trading day. A short earns when the price falls, so the gain is (open − close) / open
c
the cost per transaction as a share of the amount traded, here 0.10%. Every krone is traded twice, short and buy-back, so the cost is 2c

Example: 9 December 2025. On 8 December, Hexagon Composites rose 10.5% and Wallenius Wilhelmsen 5.2%, so both are shorted at the open on the 9th, with half the money each.

StockOpenCloseGain on the short
Hexagon Composites8.037.50+6.60%
Wallenius Wilhelmsen101.80104.90−3.05%

R = ½ × (6.60% − 3.05%) − 2 × 0.10% = 1.78% − 0.20% = +1.58%. Hexagon fell back after its jump while Wallenius kept rising, and the day still made money because the gain on Hexagon was larger.

Only liquid stocks are used: a median daily turnover of at least NOK 5 million over the previous three months and a share price of at least NOK 1. Prices are adjusted for dividends and splits.

Trading costs decide it

  • Commission is small for a professional, often well under 0.05% per trade.
  • The spread between bid and ask is the real cost. For the large stocks it is a few hundredths of a per cent, but the stocks that jump 5% are often small and volatile, where it can be 0.2–0.5%.
  • Borrowing the shares to short them costs a fee, and the most volatile stocks are often hard or impossible to borrow on the day you want them.
  • The opening price is set in an auction. Shorting a large amount there moves the price against you.

What the backtest leaves out

  • Borrowing. No borrowing fee is included, and the test assumes every stock could be borrowed and shorted at the open.
  • Size. Results are per krone invested. Large positions in small stocks would move prices.
  • Survivorship. Only companies still listed today have data, the same limitation as in the risk project.
  • Hindsight. The 5% threshold and the five years were chosen before seeing the results, but trying many thresholds and keeping the best would overstate what could be earned.

This is a backtest for learning, not investment advice. Short selling can lose more than the money put in, and past results say little about future results.

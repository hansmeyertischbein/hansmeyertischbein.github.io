Taylor rule for Norges Bank

A simple rule sets the interest rate from two things: how far inflation is from target and how hard the economy is running. This page compares what the rule says with the rate Norges Bank actually set, from 2015 until today.

What goes into the rule

Inflation is CPI-ATE, consumer prices adjusted for tax changes and excluding energy, which is the measure Norges Bank watches most closely. The output gap says how far mainland GDP is above or below its trend, in percent.

How the rule works

John Taylor showed in 1993 that a short formula described US monetary policy surprisingly well. In its usual form:

i = r* + π + a(π − π*) + b·y

  • i is the policy rate the rule recommends.
  • r* is the neutral real rate: the real rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. Norges Bank estimates it at 0.25% to 1.75%. The default here is the middle, 1%.
  • π is inflation (CPI-ATE) and π* the target: 2.5% until March 2018 and 2% since.
  • y is the output gap.
  • a and b are the weights. Taylor used 0.5 on both, so the rate rises 1.5 points for each point of extra inflation. That more-than-one-for-one response is the Taylor principle: it raises the real rate when inflation rises.

With smoothing, the rule only moves part of the way each quarter: i = ρ·(last quarter's actual rate) + (1 − ρ)·(rule). Central banks change rates gradually, so a smoothed rule usually tracks reality better.

How the output gap is estimated

This page estimates the gap itself from Statistics Norway's seasonally adjusted mainland GDP, so every step can be checked and repeated. The trend is a Hodrick-Prescott filter: the standard setting (λ = 1,600) and a smoother trend (λ = 40,000). The filter is unreliable at the end of a series, so GDP is first extended with Norges Bank's growth projections from the latest Monetary Policy Report, and the extension is dropped afterwards. The most recent quarter uses the previous quarter's gap until new GDP figures are out.

What the rule leaves out

  • The krone. A weaker krone pushes up import prices, and Norges Bank takes that into account. The rule does not.
  • Forecasts. Norges Bank sets the rate from where it expects inflation and activity to go, not where they were last quarter.
  • Risk. When the economy shut down in March 2020, the bank cut the rate to zero within eight weeks, far below what the rule says for that year.
  • The gap itself is uncertain and gets revised when new GDP figures arrive, sometimes by more than half a point.

Norges Bank uses its own Taylor rule as one of several cross-checks, with weights estimated on Norwegian data and its own output gap. The rule on this page is the textbook version, so it will not match the bank's charts exactly.

How the model works

An insurer’s balance sheet is its business. Premiums are collected before claims are paid, the money in between is invested, and regulators decide how much capital must stand behind the risk. Book equity is therefore a meaningful starting point for value, and the question becomes how much more than book the equity is worth.

The residual income model answers that. A company is worth its book equity plus the present value of the profits it earns above what shareholders require. If return on equity, cost of equity and growth stay constant, the model collapses to one ratio:

P / B = (ROE − g) ÷ (r − g)

A company that earns exactly its cost of equity is worth book value. Every point of ROE above the cost of equity adds value, and growth multiplies the effect, but only when ROE is above r. Growth at a return below the cost of equity destroys value.

The same assumptions also pin down the dividend. If growth is funded by retained profit, the share of profit paid out must be 1 − g ÷ ROE. With 28% ROE and 3% growth that is about 89%, close to Gjensidige’s policy of paying out at least 80% of profit over time plus excess capital. That consistency check is a useful sign the inputs hang together.

The model can also be run backwards. Given the share price, it shows which ROE the market must expect at a chosen cost of equity, or which cost of equity investors accept at a chosen ROE. That is often more useful than a single price target, because it turns a valuation into a question you can argue about: is a permanently higher ROE realistic, or are investors simply accepting a lower return for a stable, well-capitalised insurer?

What it shows for Gjensidige

At the July 2026 reference price, Gjensidige trades at more than six times book equity. With an 8% cost of equity and 3% growth, that price requires a long-run ROE in the mid-30s, above the company’s own target of more than 28%. Put differently, if ROE settles at the target, the price implies investors accept a cost of equity below 7%.

Neither reading is unreasonable. Gjensidige has delivered an ROE above 27% in 2025 and 33% in the first half of 2026, holds a leading position in Norway, and pays out most of what it earns. Low volatility and a steady dividend can justify a lower required return than the market average. But the gap shows how much of the share price rests on profitability staying exceptionally high for a long time.

Limitations

  • The model assumes one ROE and one growth rate forever. Real returns move with the insurance cycle, weather, interest rates and competition.
  • Gjensidige reports ROE on average equity including Tier 1 capital, while the book value here excludes it, so the figures are not perfectly comparable.
  • Book equity falls each spring when the dividend is paid and rises through the year, so the price-to-book ratio depends on the date.
  • The beta of 0.8 is an assumption, not an estimate. Test other values in the CAPM calculator.

This is an analysis exercise, not investment advice.

Market analysis: Norwegian grocery retail

Three groups sell almost everything Norwegians buy in grocery stores, and the low-price chains keep taking a larger share. The change to watch this autumn is political: the government wants the leaders to open their wholesale systems to small challengers.

Key points

  • Traditional grocery stores sold NOK 238.5 billion in 2025, about 5% more than in 2024 [2]. Food prices rose 5.3% in the same year [1], so volumes were roughly flat.
  • NorgesGruppen, Coop and Rema 1000 hold about 97% of the market between them [6]. That gives an HHI of about 3,300, far above the level usually called highly concentrated.
  • Low-price stores account for two thirds of turnover [2], and Kiwi is now the largest chain in the country [3].
  • On 18 September 2026 the government proposed that wholesalers owned by the large groups must supply small retailers on reasonable terms [4]. The consultation closes on 18 December.
  • The NOK 4.9 billion fine for exchanging price information was upheld in August 2025, and the chains are suing the state [1].

The market

The market here is grocery sales through physical grocery stores in Norway, which the industry calls traditional grocery. It made up 85% of a total grocery market of about NOK 280 billion in 2025. The rest went through kiosks and petrol stations, discount variety stores and online shops [2].

Growth in the last few years has come from prices rather than from people buying more. Food prices rose 11.5% in 2022, 9.0% in 2023, 4.0% in 2024 and 5.3% in 2025 [1]. Shoppers have responded by moving to low-price stores: their share of turnover is up more than five percentage points since 2020 by the Competition Authority’s measure [1].

The groups compete on price first, through campaigns and their own budget ranges, then on location. Location matters because leases have often locked rivals out: in twelve areas the Competition Authority studied, about 15% of leases still had exclusivity clauses in 2025, although NorgesGruppen has removed half of its clauses there since 2020 [1]. Newcomers are small. The discount variety chain Normal opened 13 stores, Fudi has two, and Foodora and Wolt deliver groceries [1].

PESTEL

How each factor affects the established grocery groups over the next two to three years.

Political

High impactHeadwind

Grocery prices are a political issue, and the government has moved from reports to rules. The wholesale access proposal would let small retailers buy from the big groups’ wholesalers on reasonable terms, including private label [4]. The government chose not to regulate purchase prices in 2025 [1], so the pressure goes through access and transparency instead.

Economic

High impactMixed

Inflation keeps turnover rising, but costs rise too: NorgesGruppen’s operating margin fell to 3.3% [3]. Price-conscious shoppers move to low-price chains and budget ranges, which helps Kiwi and Rema 1000 and squeezes the full-range stores.

Social

Medium impactMixed

Years of price rises and a large fine for price coordination have probably cost the chains some trust, which makes shoppers more willing to switch. Private label is still a smaller part of sales than in Europe, 24% against close to 40% [1], so there is room to grow if shoppers keep trading down.

Technological

Low impactMixed

Online grocery is only 2.3% of the market [2], and delivery apps have not changed how most people shop. Technology matters more inside the business, in pricing, logistics and loyalty programmes, than as a threat from new channels.

Environmental

Medium impactHeadwind

The new food waste act will require every food business to plan and publish measures against waste, and shops must mark down perishable food [7]. Grocery stores escaped a duty to donate surplus food, so the cost is mainly reporting and markdowns, but the national goal of halving food waste by 2030 keeps the pressure on.

Legal

High impactHeadwind

The Competition Authority took over enforcement of the good trading practice act in May 2026 [1], and a bill would add fines of up to 1% of turnover and limits on copying suppliers’ products in private label [4]. The outcome of the chains’ lawsuit over the NOK 4.9 billion fine will set the tone for years.

SWOT: NorgesGruppen

The market leader has the most to gain from the current model and the most to lose from the new rules.

Strengths

  • About 43.5% of the market and the largest chain, Kiwi [3][6]
  • Lower purchase prices than Coop and Rema 1000 [1]
  • Owns its wholesale and distribution
  • Profitable at scale, with NOK 4.99bn in operating profit in 2025 [3]

Weaknesses

  • Operating margin down from 3.6% to 3.3% as costs rose [3]
  • Part of the price coordination case and the lawsuit that follows [1]
  • Part of its edge rests on lease terms that are being removed [1]

Opportunities

  • Pharmacies through Vitusapotek extend the store network into health [3]
  • Shoppers trading down favour Kiwi and the First Price range [3]
  • Private label share well below the European level [1]

Threats

  • Wholesale access would give challengers its range and logistics [4]
  • Fines of up to 1% of turnover and limits on private label imitation [4]
  • Discount variety stores such as Normal taking everyday purchases [1]

What to watch

  1. Autumn 2026: the court hearing in the chains’ lawsuit against the state over the NOK 4.9 billion fine [1].
  2. 18 December 2026: the consultation deadline for the wholesale access regulation, and how hard the three groups push back [5].
  3. When the Storting votes: the changes to the good trading practice act, Prop. 112 L, and whether the fines and limits on imitation survive [4].

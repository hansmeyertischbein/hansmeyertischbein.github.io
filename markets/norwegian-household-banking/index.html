Market analysis: Norwegian household banking

Competition, not the policy rate, now sets earnings in Norwegian household banking: in the past year lower mortgage prices cost DNB about NOK 2 billion a year, about twice what it says a 25 basis point rate move adds back. The banks that come out ahead are those that cut costs and gather deposits rather than buy mortgage growth, which puts SpareBank 1 Sør-Norge ahead of DNB and DNB ahead of Sparebanken Norge.

Key points

  • Price cuts, not the rate cycle, are taking DNB’s household margin. Its mortgage spread fell 0.59 points in a year while its deposit spread rose only 0.39, so about 0.2 points a year is lower prices, or about NOK 2 billion a year on NOK 984.7 billion of loans (own calculation from [1][2], method below). DNB puts a 25 basis point rate move at NOK 0.8 to 1.2 billion [4].
  • Growth does not protect against it. DNB grew average household loans 1.5%, SpareBank 1 Sør-Norge its loans 6.7% and Sparebanken Norge 8.2%, yet each earned about a tenth less net interest income than it would have at last year’s margin, equal to 11% to 15% of pre-tax profit (own calculations from [1][5][6][7]).
  • The household margin now lives in deposits. In August, before the hike distorted the figures, a new mortgage earned about 0.56% over NIBOR and a household deposit about 1.62% (own calculations from SSB [9]). That mortgage spread is the 0.6% level which Finance Sweden says gave Swedish mortgage lenders a 3% return on equity in 2025 [17].
  • Banks and DNB expect the September hike to help in the fourth quarter, and this analysis agrees [14][4]. It differs on 2027: the repricing closes a lag, while price competition of about 0.2 points a year on DNB’s mortgages is not a lag and does not reverse.
  • Costs and deposits, not loan growth, will separate the listed banks. SpareBank 1 Sør-Norge cut quarterly costs by NOK 106 million and grew deposits 11.6% against loans 6.7% [23]; Sparebanken Norge’s costs rose NOK 54 million and its deposits grew 5.2% against loans 8.2% [7].

The market

The market is lending to and taking deposits from Norwegian households at banks and their mortgage companies, in kroner. Nearly all of it floats: 95.8% of household loans had no or short rate fixation in early 2026 [21], so banks reset prices themselves after each policy rate change.

Volume is growing while income per krone falls. SSB reports that all banks’ net interest income through the second quarter of 2026 was 3.7% lower than a year earlier while loans to customers grew 2.5% [11], about 6% less income per krone lent (own calculation: 0.963 / 1.025). That figure mixes competition with a lower rate level, since Norges Bank cut in June and September 2025, to 4.00% [22][24], and only raised the rate back to 4.25% in May 2026 [15]. The banks’ own spreads separate the two.

The combined line mixes price with timing. When NIBOR moves before banks reprice, mortgage and deposit spreads move by about the same amount in opposite directions, and because DNB lends more than it takes in, the combined spread also dips. From the third quarter of 2025 to the third of 2026, DNB’s lending spread fell 0.59 points and its deposit spread rose 0.39 [1]; from the second quarter of 2025, less affected by the cut that September, the figures are 0.43 and 0.16 [2][1]. The part of the mortgage decline not mirrored on the deposit side, 0.20 points over four quarters and 0.27 over five, about 0.22 a year, is the best estimate of lower prices, assuming DNB’s deposit pricing was unchanged (own estimate). DNB itself names “repricing effects and competition” [1], and Nordea says competition “possibly intensified a little bit in Norway in Q3” [8].

The mortgage itself is now thin by Nordic standards. Finance Sweden, the Swedish banks’ association, puts the margin of the three largest Swedish mortgage lenders at 0.6% in 2025, which it says gave a 3% return on equity, against 10% at 0.9% [17]. SSB’s Norwegian figure for new mortgages was 0.34% over NIBOR in September, but that reflects the hike: NIBOR rose 0.20 points while mortgage rates barely moved. In August it was about 0.56% (own calculation: 0.34 + 0.22 [9]). The Swedish measure deducts actual funding costs, so the comparison is approximate.

The paradox is concentration. Menon Economics, writing for the homeowners’ association Huseierne, finds that the five largest banks and alliances hold 82% of registered mortgages and that 296 of 350 municipalities are above the HHI level at which the EU examines mergers [16]. National shares are not published, so a national HHI cannot be calculated. On Finance Sweden’s measure of the total credit market, Norway is the least concentrated in the Nordics: the four largest banks hold 53%, against 64% in Sweden, 75% in Denmark (older data) and 82% in Finland [17]. Loyal customers protect the back book locally (only 9% switched mortgage bank in 2025 [16]), while merged savings banks fight nationally for the new loans that set the front book price.

Different strategies gave the same margin result; funding is where they differ. Deposits cover 67% of DNB’s household loans, 56% of SpareBank 1 Sør-Norge’s and 45% of Sparebanken Norge’s, and only SpareBank 1 Sør-Norge is growing deposits faster than loans [1][23][7]. A new mortgage funded in the covered bond market earns the mortgage spread less the bond spread over NIBOR, so at spreads near 0.56% the marginal wholesale funded loan adds little income.

Investor view

The household margin is falling faster than a rate move can restore it, so 2027 earnings growth has to come from costs, deposits, fees and capital return. DNB’s household year in NOK billion a year (own calculations from [1]): narrower spreads −3.2, of which price competition about −2.0 and the repricing lag on loans not funded by deposits about −1.2 (0.39 points × NOK 326.4bn); higher volumes +0.5; other items +0.6 (residual); reported net interest income −2.2. The lag part reverses with the November repricing; the competition part does not.

The ranking survives the base case. If spreads hold at the third quarter level plus the November repricing, Sparebanken Norge’s faster growth would add the most income, about NOK 0.9bn a year at 8.2% (own calculation from [6][7]). Two things outweigh that: its growth is funded more in the bond market, where the marginal mortgage earns little, and its costs are rising, while SpareBank 1 Sør-Norge funds growth with deposits and cuts NOK 0.42bn a year of costs [23]. DNB sits in between: the largest absolute loss, but the most deposits per krone lent and growing non-interest income.

PESTEL

Effect on household banking margins over the next two to three years.

Political

Low impactHeadwind

In June 2026 four parties in the Storting’s finance committee asked for regional measures of bank competition, citing five banks and chains with over 80% of mortgages [18]. The proposals were in the minority and no process with a date follows from them, so the near term effect on margins is small; the risk is slower approval of future mergers.

Economic

High impactMixed

The rate level drives deposit spreads, which now carry the household margin. Norges Bank raised the rate twice in 2026 and expects it to stay elevated before declining somewhat [12]; each 25 bp is worth NOK 0.8 to 1.2bn to DNB [4]. It turns against the banks when cuts come, and household debt growth has slowed to 4.5% [10].

Social

Medium impactTailwind

Customers rarely move. Only 9% of mortgage customers switched in 2025, down from 11% in 2024 [16]. Inertia shields the back book of the largest lender, DNB with 22.1% of household credit [1], while the growing savings banks win the front book; that is why the margin falls gradually rather than all at once.

Technological

Medium impactMixed

Digital sales make the mortgage a gateway to fees: Nordea links a 20% rise in Norwegian savings fees to selling savings inside mortgage onboarding [8]. DNB says it is preparing for AI driven products to change competition [4], which could make switching easier.

Environmental

Low impactMixed

No environmental rule in the sources used moves household spreads in 2026 or 2027. The factor works through the housing that secures the loans, a credit question, and third quarter losses were small: NOK 85m and 97m at the two savings banks [5][6].

Legal

Medium impactHeadwind

A pending rule bears directly on the thesis. In February 2025 the Ministry of Finance consulted on Finanstilsynet’s proposal to ban requiring a salary account for better mortgage terms; the ministry lists it as under consideration [19]. It would weaken the link that turns a thin margin mortgage into deposits. The lending regulation caps volume: 7.7% of new mortgages outside Oslo breached a requirement in the second quarter, the highest since 2020 [20].

SWOT: DNB

Strengths

  • 22.1% of household credit and 28.2% of household savings [1]
  • Household deposits equal 67% of household loans (own calculation, [1])
  • 39.4% of Norwegian mutual fund savings, a source of fees beyond lending [1]
  • Household non-interest income up NOK 181m in a year, to NOK 2,270m a quarter [1]

Weaknesses

  • Mortgage spread of 0.61%, down from 1.20% a year earlier [1]
  • Average household loans grew 1.5%, below household debt growth of 4.5% [1][10]
  • Group cost to income up from 37.4% to 39.8% [3]

Opportunities

  • Repricing of existing loans and deposits from 29 November [1]
  • A rate held “elevated for a time” keeps deposit spreads wide [12]
  • Selling savings to mortgage customers, as Nordea does in Norway [8]

Threats

  • Merged savings banks expanding nationally [22][6]
  • A pending ban on salary account conditions for mortgages [19]
  • Rate cuts that compress deposit spreads [12]

Risks to the view

  1. Competition fades once the mergers are digested. Front book prices could recover if the savings banks shift from growth to returns. Signal: their loan growth falling toward household debt growth of 4.5%, and DNB’s household lending spread back above 0.9% at a stable NIBOR.
  2. Rates rise further. Norges Bank says it is prepared to raise the rate again [12]. Two more hikes would add NOK 1.6 to 2.4bn at DNB on its own range [4], close to a year of competitive loss. Signal: a hike on 5 November or 17 December, or a rate path above 4.5% through 2027.
  3. Sparebanken Norge’s new markets bring deposits. Growth funded by deposits would earn more than assumed here and change the ranking. Signal: deposit growth above lending growth; in the third quarter it was 5.2% against 8.2% [7].

What to watch

  1. 5 November 2026: Norges Bank’s rate decision, without a new forecast [13]. A hike would widen deposit spreads further.
  2. 27 to 29 November 2026: SSB’s October interest rate statistics on 27 November [9], then DNB’s higher rates for existing customers from 29 November [1]. If the mortgage margin over NIBOR does not recover from 0.35% in the releases that follow the repricing, the competitive loss is larger than the lag.
  3. 17 December 2026: Norges Bank’s Monetary Policy Report with a new rate path [13]. An earlier path of cuts would bring forward the squeeze on deposit spreads.

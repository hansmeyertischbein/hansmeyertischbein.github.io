Market analysis: Norwegian salmon farming

Norway’s salmon exports are up about 10% in volume this year on almost the same licences, and the state’s 2026 capacity round is worth less to any large farmer than a NOK 1 per kg move in the salmon price. Earnings from here will be decided by price, cost and biology rather than by licences. If prices firm in 2027 as brokers expect, Mowi, with falling costs and rising earnings, is the lower-risk way to gain; Lerøy, whose harvest and earnings are down, is the most leveraged to the price either way.

Key points

  • Volume, not price, carried export growth. In 2025 export volume rose 13% while value rose about 1.8%, so the average export price fell about 10%. In the first nine months of 2026 volume rose about 10% and value about 6% (own calculations from the NOK amounts in [10][11][12]).
  • Licences explain little of that growth. The 2022 auction and the 2024 round added about 34,000 tonnes of capacity, worth about 4 to 5% of the 2025 harvest even at a generous two tonnes of harvest per tonne (own calculation from [26][23]). The Seafood Council credits favourable biology [12].
  • The 2026 round is too small to move earnings. On generous assumptions SalMar’s share is worth about NOK 93 million a year before tax, Mowi’s NOK 14 million, and Lerøy loses about NOK 45 million. A NOK 1 per kg change in the price is worth NOK 164 to 332 million to each of them (own calculations from [1][3][15]).
  • Brokers expect slower supply and firmer prices in 2027. On 20 October Arctic Securities forecast Norwegian supply down 3.5% and a price rising from NOK 75 in 2026 to about NOK 85; in April and May Pareto and SEB expected NOK 80 rising to about 86, with Pareto seeing global growth below 1% [24][27][28]. This analysis agrees: this year’s biology-driven volume is unlikely to repeat, and licences cannot replace it.
  • Mowi is the lower-risk way to own that view, Lerøy the most leveraged. In the second quarter Mowi’s operational EBIT rose 23% as its farming cost fell, while Lerøy’s fell 16% on lower harvest; but a NOK 1 per kg price move is 7.8% of Lerøy’s quarterly earnings against 5.9% of Mowi’s ([22][5], own calculation).

The market

The market here is the farming of Atlantic salmon in sea pens along the Norwegian coast, sold at harvest to processors, exporters and retailers, most of them abroad. It excludes trout, land based farming and feed. About 100 companies own commercial licences [1].

Revenue growth has come from volume while prices fell. Norway exported 1,414,909 tonnes of salmon worth NOK 124.7 billion in 2025, 13% more volume for NOK 2.2 billion more value [10]. That is value growth of about 1.8% and an average export price of about NOK 88 per kg, roughly 10% below 2024 (own calculations; the Council rounds the value growth to 2%). The pattern held in the first half of 2026, when volume rose 8% and value about 0.6% [11]. The third quarter was the first period in which value grew faster than volume: 464,700 tonnes worth NOK 36.2 billion, 12% more volume and about 14.6% more value, even though the krone was stronger in September than a year earlier [12].

The extra fish came mostly from existing licences. The 2022 auction sold 24,644 tonnes of new capacity [26], and the 2024 traffic light round offered about 21,000 tonnes of growth and cut about 11,400 [23], a net of about 9,600 (own calculation). Even if every tonne yielded two tonnes of harvest a year, a generous assumption, the two rounds add about 4 to 5% to the 1.53 million tonnes harvested in 2025 (own calculation: 34,244 × 2 ÷ 1,534,300; fixed-price sales in 2022 and exemption growth are not included), against export volume growth of 13% in 2025 and about 10% so far in 2026. Biology did more. The Seafood Council’s analyst attributes September’s record to “favourable biological conditions throughout 2026” [12], and lower mortality alone meant about 1.4% more surviving fish in 2025 (own calculation from survival of 84.6% and 85.8%, [17]). By subtraction, the rest came from more biomass grown on the same licences, a gain that does not compound.

Brokers now expect the volume to slow or reverse. On 20 October Arctic Securities forecast that Norwegian supply will fall 3.5% in 2027 and global supply 1%, cut its 2026 price to NOK 75.1 per kg (EUR 6.95) and raised 2027 to about EUR 7.90, about NOK 85 at the same exchange rate (own calculation), citing strong demand as well [24]. Earlier forecasts, made before most of this year’s volume was known, were higher for 2026: SEB in May expected NOK 80 and NOK 86 for 2027 [27], and Pareto in April NOK 80 recovering towards NOK 86, with global supply growth below 1% in 2027 [28]. This analysis uses Arctic’s as the most recent. It shares the brokers’ direction for its own reason: the 2026 gain came from biology on fixed licences, so it cannot be repeated by adding capacity.

Norway is also the most profitable origin. Mowi earned about EUR 1.80 per kg on Norwegian salmon through the value chain in the third quarter, against EUR 1.70 for Scottish, EUR 1.25 for western Canadian and EUR 0.55 for Chilean salmon [2].

Capacity is set by the state. Under the traffic light system the coast is split into 13 production areas, coloured by how sea lice from farms affect wild salmon. In 2026 areas 1, 12 and 13 are green and may grow by up to 6%, nine areas are yellow and unchanged, and area 3, Karmøy to Sotra, is red and must cut 6% [13][15]. The final regulation offers 8,200 tonnes of growth in green areas, one percentage point at a fixed NOK 270,000 per tonne and the rest by auction this autumn, while area 3 loses an estimated 5,300 tonnes. Farms with very low lice emissions may apply for up to 6% exemption growth in any area [14].

Ownership is spread out. The ten largest farmers harvested 1,120,000 of the 1,534,300 tonnes in 2025, about 73%, and the four largest, Mowi, SalMar, Lerøy and Cermaq, about 58% [1]. Their squared shares give an HHI of about 1,000 (own calculation from the shares in [1]), and the price is set in a global market, so no farmer has pricing power. Licences cap the industry’s total supply, which supports the price for everyone, but the changes from one round to the next are too small to separate the companies.

Salmon is close to a commodity, so farmers compete on cost and biology. Mowi cut its blended farming cost by EUR 0.18 per kg in the second quarter and EUR 0.31 in the third [22][2], while ISA raised SalMar’s costs [4] and Lerøy harvested less than a year earlier in both quarters [5][6].

Investor view

The 2026 licence round does not change any listed farmer’s earnings in a way that matters: for each of them it is worth less than a NOK 1 per kg move in the salmon price. What matters is the price, and how much of it each company keeps. If prices rise by NOK 6 to 10 per kg, the range from SEB and Pareto in the spring to Arctic in October, quarterly operational EBIT at second quarter volumes and costs would rise about 47 to 78% at Lerøy, 40 to 66% at SalMar and 35 to 59% at Mowi (own calculation from the shares in the table below, assuming all volume is sold at the spot price). Lerøy has the most to gain and the most to lose; Mowi, with falling costs and rising earnings, is the lower-risk way to own a firmer price. Mowi (MOWI), SalMar (SALM), Lerøy (LSG) and Grieg Seafood (GSF) are listed on Oslo Børs; Cermaq is owned by Mitsubishi.

The same quarter shows the split. Mowi’s operational EBIT rose 23% in the second quarter as farming cost fell to EUR 5.21 from EUR 5.39 per kg [22], and more than doubled in the third [2]. Lerøy’s fell 16% on a harvest 8% lower [5], and a price move is a larger share of its earnings than for its peers, so it has less cushion if prices fall. SalMar sits between: record biology in the second quarter [3], ISA costs in the third [4]. Margins per kg in the second quarter show why: about NOK 20 for Mowi’s Norwegian salmon through the value chain (EUR 1.85 at the quarter’s exchange rate), NOK 17.2 for SalMar in Norway, and about NOK 5.3 for Lerøy’s farming segment, whose downstream profit sits in its sales arm (own calculations from [22][3][5]; Lerøy’s figure includes trout). The scenario overstates leverage for companies that sell part of their volume on fixed-price contracts; the third quarter reports will give those shares.

PESTEL

How each factor affects the large salmon farmers over the next two to three years.

Political

High impactHeadwind

Politicians decide how much each area may produce. In June 2025 a broad majority in the Storting asked the government to assess several models for future regulation, including lice quotas allocated by company, and to bring the choice back to the Storting [16]. A new model could move far more capacity than one traffic light round, and until it is chosen farmers cannot know what will set the value of their licences.

Economic

High impactMixed

Price is the largest swing factor in earnings, as the investor view shows. Volume growth cut the average export price about 10% in 2025 and 7% in the first half of 2026 (own calculations, [10][11]). In September prices rose in euros and dollars while a stronger krone held back the NOK value [12]. The United States has charged a 15% tariff on Norwegian seafood since 7 August 2025 [21], and the value of fresh fillets sent there fell 38% in the first half of 2026 [11].

Social

Medium impactMixed

Raw salmon is already a habit in Asia’s largest market: about a quarter of seafood consumers in China’s largest cities eat salmon sashimi weekly or more, twice the 13% average across 20 markets in the Seafood Council’s consumer tracker [25], and Norway’s share of China’s salmon imports was the highest in 15 years in July and August [12]. Against that, 54.8 million salmon died in Norwegian sea pens in 2025 [17], which keeps fish welfare in the public debate at home and feeds back into the political factor.

Technological

Medium impactTailwind

Farming offshore is the long term way past the lice limit, but it is at an early stage. SalMar harvested its Ocean Farm 1 structure successfully in 2026 [3], and in February 2026 the state set the outer limits of the sea areas that can be offered for offshore farming, a step before any allocation [20]. Meanwhile existing farms depend on more treatment: lice treatments reached a record in 2025, including almost 4,000 treatment weeks of non-medicinal treatment [17].

Environmental

High impactHeadwind

Biology drove this year’s volume and can reverse it. Area 3 must cut about 5,300 tonnes in 2026 because of lice [14], Grieg harvested early after an algae event [7], and SalMar had to harvest fish because of ISA [4]. Each such event hits volume and cost at once.

Legal

Low impactHeadwind

Since 2023 salmon farming has paid a resource rent tax with an effective rate of 25%, on top of corporate tax [18]. The Storting voted on 5 June 2026 to abolish the norm price council from 2027, but the government will keep it for 2027 and plans a consultation in spring 2027, iLaks reported [19]. The rate is settled, so what remains is method, with little effect on profit.

SWOT: Mowi

The market leader shows best how cost, not capacity, drives earnings when growth is rationed.

Strengths

  • Largest farmer in Norway, 21.6% of 2025 harvest [1]
  • Farming cost down from EUR 5.42 to EUR 5.11 per kg in a year [2]
  • Norwegian salmon earned EUR 1.80 per kg through the value chain in Q3, its best origin [2]
  • Operational EBIT up 23% in Q2 and more than doubled in Q3 [22][2]

Weaknesses

  • Chilean salmon earned only EUR 0.55 per kg in Q3 [2]
  • Norway is about 85% of the EBIT it reports for its four largest origins (own calculation from [2]), so Norwegian rules and prices drive the group
  • Loss-making in Iceland (EUR −0.40 per kg) and Ireland (EUR −0.15 per kg) in Q2 2026 [22]

Opportunities

  • Firmer prices if 2027 supply falls as Arctic forecasts [24][28]
  • Record Chinese demand for Norwegian salmon [12]
  • Four closed pens ordered to bring 2.6 withdrawn licences in Western Norway back into use [29]

Threats

  • Lower NOK prices when industry volumes rise, as in 2025 [10]
  • A new regulatory model that reallocates capacity [16]
  • Disease, algae and warmer sea conditions [4][7]

Risks to the view

  1. Better biology lasts. If mortality keeps falling, Norway can keep growing output without new licences, and 2027 prices would not get the support of slower supply. Signal: the Veterinary Institute’s fish health report for 2026, due around March 2027, and first half 2027 export volumes above those of 2026.
  2. Prices turn down again. The third quarter was the first period in which value grew faster than volume, and a stronger krone is already reducing NOK values [12]. Lerøy would be hit hardest. Signal: monthly Seafood Council figures with value growth back below volume growth.
  3. Licences start to matter. Lice quotas allocated by company [16] could move far more capacity than a traffic light round and reward farms with low lice wherever they are. That would make licences a real driver of relative earnings. Signal: the government’s assessment of regulatory models when it is sent to the Storting.

What to watch

  1. 3 to 12 November 2026: third quarter reports from SalMar on 3 November [4], Mowi on 4 November [2], Lerøy on 10 November [6] and Grieg around 12 November [7]. Compare cost and EBIT per kg on the same quarter, and look for 2027 volume guidance. Lerøy’s farming margin tests the relative view.
  2. Early November 2026: the Seafood Council’s export figures for October, the next test of whether value keeps growing faster than volume [12].
  3. Autumn 2026, date not yet announced: the auction of the green area growth not sold at the fixed price [14]. The state’s fixed price has risen from NOK 170,000 per tonne in 2024 [23] to NOK 270,000. Bids well above that would show farmers expect today’s margins to last; bids at or below it would show the opposite.

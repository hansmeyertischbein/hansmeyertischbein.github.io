Market analysis: Norwegian salmon farming

Norway’s salmon exports are up about 10% in volume this year, although the state has added almost no farming capacity. Better biology, not new licences, is behind the boom, and because that is hard to repeat inside a fixed licence system, earnings from 2027 will depend on cost and on licences in the few areas allowed to grow, which favours SalMar and Mowi over Lerøy.

Key points

  • Volume, not price, carried export growth. In 2025 export volume rose 13% and value 1.8%, so the average export price fell about 10%. In the first nine months of 2026 volume rose about 10% and value about 6% (own calculation from [10][11][12]).
  • The extra volume came from biology, not from new capacity. Mortality at sea fell to 14.2% in 2025 [17], the Seafood Council links September’s record volume to good biological conditions all year [12], and the 2026 traffic light round adds a net of only about 2,900 tonnes of allowed biomass (own calculation from [14]).
  • Mowi’s outlook of 1% a year in Norwegian supply growth from 2025 to 2027 [1] implies about 6% less supply in 2027 than in 2026 if this year ends near +9% (own calculation). That supports prices but takes away the volume that carried 2026.
  • The capacity rules pick winners. DNB Carnegie estimates that SalMar gains about 2,700 tonnes of allowed biomass from the 2026 round and Mowi a net 400 tonnes, while Lerøy loses about 1,300 tonnes in the red area 3 [15].
  • Margins already reward the cost leaders. Mowi’s Norwegian farms earned EUR 1.80 per kg in the third quarter against EUR 0.55 in Chile, after a cut in farming cost of EUR 0.31 per kg in a year [2].

The market

The market here is the farming of Atlantic salmon in sea pens along the Norwegian coast, sold at harvest to processors, exporters and retailers, most of them abroad. It excludes trout, land based farming and feed. About 100 companies own commercial licences for salmon and trout, and supply comes from around 90 companies directly or through subsidiaries [1].

Revenue growth has come from volume while prices fell. Norway exported 1,414,909 tonnes of salmon worth NOK 124.7 billion in 2025, 13% more volume for only NOK 2.2 billion, or 1.8%, more value [10]. The average export price was therefore about NOK 88 per kg, roughly 10% below 2024 (own calculation). The pattern held in the first half of 2026, when volume rose 8% and value 0.6% [11]. The third quarter was the first period in which value grew faster than volume: 464,700 tonnes worth NOK 36.2 billion, up 12% and 14.6%, even though the krone was stronger in September than a year earlier [12].

The extra fish did not come from extra licences. The Seafood Council’s analyst attributes September’s record volume to “favourable biological conditions throughout 2026” [12], and the Norwegian Veterinary Institute reports that mortality at sea fell from about 15.4% to 14.2% in 2025 [17]. Fewer dead fish and faster growth on the same licences raise output for a while, but they are not a source of growth that compounds.

The market itself does not expect it to last. Mowi’s 2026 industry handbook forecasts Norwegian supply growth of 1% a year from 2025 to 2027, against 2% globally [1]. Using exports as a proxy for harvest, a 2026 that ends about 9% above 2025 would leave 2027 about 6% below 2026 under that forecast (own calculation: 1.01² ÷ 1.09). That is the main reason to expect firmer prices and flat or lower volumes next year.

Norway is also the most profitable place to farm salmon. In Mowi’s third quarter, Norway earned EUR 1.80 per kg, against EUR 1.70 in Scotland, EUR 1.25 in western Canada and EUR 0.55 in Chile [2]. That gap is why a tonne of Norwegian licence capacity is valuable, and why the rules that allocate it matter more than competition for customers.

Capacity is set by the state. Under the traffic light system the coast is split into 13 production areas, coloured by how sea lice from farms affect wild salmon. In 2026 areas 1, 12 and 13 are green and may grow by up to 6%, nine areas are yellow and unchanged, and area 3, Karmøy to Sotra, is red and must cut 6% for the third time [13]. The final regulation offers 8,200 tonnes of growth in the green areas at a fixed price of NOK 270,000 per tonne for part of it and by auction for the rest, while area 3 loses about 5,300 tonnes [14]. Net of the cut, the round adds about 2,900 tonnes of maximum allowed biomass before exemption growth, and at the state’s own price the cut in area 3 removes capacity worth about NOK 1.4 billion (own calculations: 8,200 − 5,300 tonnes; 5,300 × NOK 270,000).

Ownership is spread out. The ten largest farmers harvested 1,120,000 of the 1,534,300 tonnes in 2025, about 73%, and the four largest, Mowi, SalMar, Lerøy and Cermaq, about 58% [1]. Their squared shares give an HHI of about 1,000 (own calculation from the shares in [1]), below the 1,500 usually called unconcentrated, and the remaining 27% is spread over many small farmers. The binding constraint on the industry is the licence system, not market power.

Salmon is close to a commodity, so farmers compete on cost and biology rather than on price. Mowi cut its farming cost by EUR 0.31 per kg in a year, worth EUR 51 million in the third quarter alone [2], while ISA raised SalMar’s costs in Central Norway [4]. Lerøy shows the other side: its farming segment earned NOK 236 million in the second quarter, less than its sales arm’s NOK 269 million [5], and its volumes are falling. The other contest is for capacity. Buying farms in green areas, as Cermaq did in Finnmark, is the fastest way to grow when new licences are scarce.

Investor view

Mowi (MOWI), SalMar (SALM), Lerøy (LSG) and Grieg Seafood (GSF) are listed on Oslo Børs; Cermaq is owned by Mitsubishi. For the first two, Norwegian farming is most of the earnings the thesis touches. Mowi’s Norwegian farms earned about EUR 241 million in the third quarter, close to nine tenths of the group’s EUR 275 million operational EBIT (own calculation: 134,000 t × EUR 1.80 per kg) [2]. SalMar harvested 99,900 of its 105,100 tonnes in Norway in the same quarter [4]. Lerøy depends less on farming, as its segment results show [5].

The view is relative. If 2027 brings flatter volumes and firmer prices, every farmer gets the price, but only those with growing licences and falling costs also get volume and margin. SalMar gains the most capacity and reported record biological indicators before the ISA cases in Central Norway [3][4][15]. Mowi cut its farming cost by EUR 0.31 per kg in a year and earns its best margin in Norway [2]. Lerøy combines falling volume with a net loss of capacity [6][15]. At the state’s fixed price of NOK 270,000 per tonne [14], the 2,700 tonnes SalMar may gain would cost about NOK 0.7 billion, and tonnes bought at auction may cost more (own calculation: 2,700 × NOK 270,000).

PESTEL

How each factor affects the large salmon farmers over the next two to three years.

Political

High impactHeadwind

Politicians decide how much each area may produce. In June 2025 a broad majority in the Storting asked the government to assess several models for future regulation, including lice quotas allocated by company, and to bring the choice back to the Storting [16]. Until then the traffic light system decides. Farmers cannot yet know which model will set the value of their licences, which makes long term investment harder to plan.

Economic

High impactMixed

Volume growth pushed the average export price down about 10% in 2025 and about 7% in the first half of 2026 (own calculations from [10][11]). In September prices rose in euros and dollars while a stronger krone held back the NOK value [12]. The United States has charged a 15% tariff on Norwegian seafood since 7 August 2025 [21], and the value of fresh fillets sent there fell 38% in the first half of 2026 [11].

Social

Medium impactMixed

Demand is broadening. Exports to China reached 98,473 tonnes worth NOK 8.5 billion in the first nine months of 2026, up 42% in volume, and Norway’s share of China’s salmon imports was the highest in 15 years in July and August [12]. Against that, 54.8 million salmon died in Norwegian sea pens in 2025 [17]. Losses on that scale keep fish welfare in the news and feed back into the political factor.

Technological

Medium impactTailwind

Farming offshore is the way past the lice limit. SalMar harvested Ocean Farm 1 successfully in 2026 with strong biological results [3], the rules were changed in January 2026 to allow larger offshore areas, and in February a royal decree set the outer limits of the areas that can be offered [20]. On existing farms, non-medicinal lice treatments reached a record of almost 4,000 treatment weeks in 2025 [17], so the payoff is still years away.

Environmental

High impactHeadwind

The effect of sea lice on wild salmon decides capacity, and area 3 must cut about 5,300 tonnes in 2026 [14]. Warmer and more variable sea conditions add biological risk: Grieg harvested early after an algae event [7], and SalMar had to harvest fish because of ISA [4]. The same biology that lifted 2026 volumes can reverse them.

Legal

Medium impactHeadwind

Since 2023 salmon farming has paid a resource rent tax with an effective rate of 25%, on top of corporate tax [18]. The Storting voted on 5 June 2026 to abolish the norm price council from 2027, but the government will keep it for 2027 and aims to propose abolition in the 2028 budget, iLaks reported [19]. The level of the tax is settled, so the remaining uncertainty is about method.

SWOT: Mowi

The market leader shows best how scale and low costs pay off when volume growth is rationed.

Strengths

  • Largest farmer in Norway, 21.6% of 2025 harvest [1]
  • Farming cost down from EUR 5.42 to EUR 5.11 per kg in a year [2]
  • Norway earned EUR 1.80 per kg in Q3 2026, its best region [2]
  • Gains a net of about 400 tonnes of allowed biomass in the 2026 round [15]

Weaknesses

  • Net interest bearing debt of about EUR 2.9bn [2]
  • Chile earned only EUR 0.55 per kg in Q3 2026 [2]
  • Norway is close to nine tenths of Q3 operational EBIT (own calculation from [2]), so Norwegian rules and prices drive the group

Opportunities

  • Growth in green areas 1 and 12 at NOK 270,000 per tonne or at auction [14][15]
  • Record Chinese demand for Norwegian salmon [12]
  • Larger offshore areas under the 2026 rules [20]

Threats

  • Cuts in area 3 offset part of its growth [15]
  • Lower NOK prices when industry volumes rise, as in 2025 [10]
  • Disease, algae and warmer sea conditions [4][7]

Risks to the view

  1. Better biology lasts. If mortality keeps falling, Norway can keep growing output without new licences, and 2027 volume would not fall as Mowi’s outlook implies. Signal: the Veterinary Institute’s fish health report for 2026, due around March 2027, and first half 2027 export volumes above those of 2026.
  2. Prices turn down again. The third quarter was the first period in this cycle in which value grew faster than volume, and a stronger krone is already reducing NOK values [12]. Signal: monthly Seafood Council releases in which value growth falls back below volume growth.
  3. The rules change in favour of other farmers. Lice quotas allocated by company [16] would reward farms with low lice wherever they are, which could weaken the green area advantage behind SalMar’s gain. Signal: the government’s assessment of regulatory models when it is sent to the Storting.

What to watch

  1. 3 to 12 November 2026: third quarter reports from SalMar on 3 November [4], Mowi on 4 November [2], Lerøy on 10 November [6] and Grieg around 12 November [7]. Watch cost per kg, the ISA effect at SalMar, Lerøy’s farming margin and the first volume guidance for 2027.
  2. Early November 2026: the Seafood Council’s export figures for October, the next test of whether value keeps growing faster than volume [12].
  3. Autumn 2026: the auction of growth capacity not sold at the fixed price of NOK 270,000 per tonne [14]. The prices paid show what farmers think a tonne of licence is worth under the current tax.

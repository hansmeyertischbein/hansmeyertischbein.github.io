Market analysis: Norwegian salmon farming

In Norwegian salmon farming the state, not demand, decides how much can be produced: growth is rationed through licences and a traffic light system based on sea lice. The large farmers are earning more in 2026 on record volumes, lower costs and strong Chinese demand, but the lice rules now decide who may keep growing.

Key points

  • Norway exported salmon worth a record NOK 124.7 billion in 2025. Volume rose about 13% but value only about 2%, because prices fell [8].
  • 2026 is a volume year. Third quarter salmon exports were 464,700 tonnes worth NOK 36.2 billion, up 12% in volume and 14% in value. August and September each set a new monthly value record [10][9].
  • Lower costs are lifting margins. Mowi had a record third quarter harvest of 186,000 tonnes and more than doubled operational EBIT to EUR 275 million, with farming costs down to EUR 5.11 per kg [2].
  • The market is not very concentrated: the ten largest farmers harvested about 73% of the volume and the HHI is about 1,000 [1]. The binding constraint is the licence system, not competition.
  • The 2026 traffic light round gives up to 6% growth in three green areas, freezes nine yellow areas and cuts capacity 6% in red area 3, Karmøy to Sotra, for the third time [12].

The market

The market here is the farming of Atlantic salmon in sea pens along the Norwegian coast, sold at harvest to processors, exporters and retailers, most of them abroad. It excludes trout, land based farming and feed. About 100 companies hold commercial licences, and supply comes from around 90 companies directly or through subsidiaries [1].

The HHI adds the squared harvest shares of the ten largest farmers, which comes to about 1,000. The remaining 27% is spread over many small farmers and adds very little [1]. Anything below 1,500 is usually called unconcentrated. The four largest, Mowi, SalMar, Lerøy and Cermaq, harvested about 58% between them.

Competition for customers is not what limits the industry. Total capacity is set by the state through licences measured in maximum allowed biomass, and through the traffic light system, which lets areas grow or forces them to shrink depending on how sea lice from farms affect wild salmon [12]. Mowi expects Norwegian supply to grow only about 1% a year from 2025 to 2027 [1]. Salmon prices are set in a global spot market, so the profitable farmer is the one with low costs, low mortality and licences in areas that are allowed to grow.

Salmon is close to a commodity, so farmers compete on cost and biology, not on price setting. The winners in 2026 are those with low cost per kg and few disease outbreaks: Mowi cut its farming cost by EUR 0.31 per kg in a year [2], while ISA raised SalMar’s costs and lowered the prices it got for fish harvested early [4]. The other contest is for growth. Buying farms in green areas, as Cermaq did in Finnmark, is the fastest way to grow in a market where new licences are scarce.

PESTEL

How each factor affects the large salmon farmers over the next two to three years.

Political

High impactHeadwind

Politicians decide how much each area may produce. A broad majority in the Storting agreed in June 2025 on a new regulatory system meant to cut lice pressure, to be presented to the Storting in 2026 and in force within two to four years. The traffic light system stays until then [14]. Until then farmers cannot know what their licences will be worth, which makes it harder to plan investments.

Economic

High impactMixed

Record volumes push prices down: in 2025 export volume rose about 13% but value only about 2% [8]. Falling costs offset this for the best farmers [2][4]. A stronger krone dampened export values in September 2026 [9], and a 15% US tariff, in force for the first full month in September 2025, has hurt exports of fresh whole salmon to the US [11].

Social

Medium impactMixed

Demand is broadening. Exports to China passed NOK 1 billion in a month for the first time in September 2026 [9]. Against that, 54.9 million salmon died in Norwegian sea pens in 2025 [15]. Mortality on that scale keeps animal welfare in the news and weakens the industry’s standing with voters, which feeds back into the political factor.

Technological

Medium impactTailwind

Technology is the way out of the lice limit. SalMar’s offshore Ocean Farm 1 finished a cycle in May 2026 with low mortality and no lice treatments [4], and the government has proposed rules to allocate whole sea areas for offshore farming [18]. Treatments on existing farms are still rising, with a record 3,918 treatment weeks in 2025 [15], so the payoff is years away.

Environmental

High impactHeadwind

The effect of sea lice on wild salmon is what decides capacity, and area 3 must cut about 5,300 tonnes in 2026 [13]. Warmer and more variable sea conditions add biological risk: Grieg had to harvest early after an algae event [6], and SalMar was hit by ISA [3]. Biology, not demand, is the most common reason a farmer misses its numbers.

Legal

Medium impactHeadwind

Since 2023 salmon farming pays a resource rent tax with an effective rate of about 25% on top of corporate tax [16], expected to raise about NOK 4 billion gross in 2026 [19]. The Storting voted to abolish the norm price council that sets the tax prices from 2027, but the government will keep it for 2027 [17]. The tax is now known and priced in, so the remaining uncertainty is about method rather than level.

SWOT: Mowi

The market leader shows best how scale and low costs pay off in a market where volume growth is rationed.

Strengths

  • Largest farmer in Norway, 21.6% of 2025 harvest [1]
  • Farming cost down from EUR 5.42 to EUR 5.11 per kg in a year [2]
  • Norway is its most profitable region, EUR 1.80 per kg in Q3 2026 [2]
  • Own feed and consumer products arms add earnings outside farming [2]

Weaknesses

  • Net interest bearing debt of about EUR 2.9bn [2]
  • Chile earned only EUR 0.55 per kg in Q3 2026, which dilutes group margins [2]
  • So large that it cannot grow much in Norway without new licences or acquisitions

Opportunities

  • Growth of up to 6% in green areas, bought at NOK 270,000 per tonne or at auction [13]
  • Record Chinese demand for Norwegian salmon [9]
  • Offshore areas if the new allocation rules are adopted [18]

Threats

  • Capacity cuts in red areas and an untested new regulatory system [12][14]
  • Lower prices as industry supply grows [8]
  • Disease and warmer sea conditions [3][6]

What to watch

  1. 3 to 12 November 2026: third quarter reports from SalMar on 3 November [3], Mowi on 4 November [2], Lerøy on 10 November [5] and Grieg around 12 November [6]. Watch cost per kg and the effect of ISA at SalMar.

  3. 2026 to 2027: the new regulatory system is due to be presented to the Storting [14], and the 2027 budget keeps the norm price council for one more year [17]. Both decide how much the licences are worth.

The ideas behind it

Diversification. The return of a mix is the weighted average of the two returns, but the risk is not, unless the assets move perfectly together. With a correlation below 1, part of each asset’s ups and downs cancel out, so the curve bends to the left: some mixes carry less risk than either asset alone. The lower the correlation, the more it bends.

The efficient frontier. Mixes on the upper part of the curve give the highest return for their level of risk. Anything below the lowest-risk point is dominated, because a mix higher up has the same risk and more return.

The best risk-adjusted mix. Once a risk-free asset exists, the best combination of the two risky assets is the one with the highest Sharpe ratio, where a straight line from the risk-free rate just touches the curve. Every investor should hold that same mix and adjust risk by adding cash or borrowing, moving along the dashed line instead of along the curve. This is the separation idea behind modern portfolio theory.

CAPM. If everyone holds the market portfolio, the only risk that earns a reward is the part that cannot be diversified away, measured by beta. The required return is then the risk-free rate plus beta times the market risk premium. It is the starting point for the cost of equity in most valuations, including the Gjensidige valuation on this site.

Limitations

Expected returns, volatilities and correlations are estimates, and the results are very sensitive to them, especially the expected returns. Correlations also tend to rise in crises, exactly when diversification is needed. Treat the output as a way to understand the trade-offs, not as a recommendation.
